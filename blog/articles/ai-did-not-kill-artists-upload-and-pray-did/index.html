AI Did Not Kill Artists. It Killed the Upload-and-Pray Strategy.

The work still matters. But one upload, one platform, and one prayer to the algorithm was never a career plan.

By Skye Vernon· Published July 30, 2026

Every other day, I see an artist post some half-ass video of their phone recording their computer screen, then turn around and complain that AI is taking their job.

No. Stop.

AI is not responsible for you having no release strategy, no website, no finished portfolio, no copyright registration plan, no audience path, no linked socials, no visual identity, and no idea what somebody is supposed to do after they see your work.

You did not lose a job. You uploaded a file and expected the internet to build a career around it.

For years, artists got away with a version of online culture where you could make something privately, toss it on Instagram or Tumblr or SoundCloud, and maybe get discovered. But even then, the artists who actually lasted were doing more than uploading. They had cover art. They had flyers. They had shows. They had music videos, collaborations, websites, merch, releases with actual dates on them. They were building a trail.

Now I find somebody's Instagram, and I cannot find their YouTube without doing CSI work. No link hub. No website. No official release page. No clear name. No consistent handle. No sign that they have ever thought past the moment somebody likes one post.

Then people act shocked when they go viral for ten minutes and nothing happens afterward.

Going viral is not a business model. It is an emergency.

If you do not already have your pages connected, your work organized, your official releases available, and somewhere for people to go next, then your viral moment just becomes a bunch of strangers briefly watching you fail to capitalize on attention.

And I am tired of pretending that is an AI problem.

Human slop is still slop

AI is absolutely changing creative work. It is going to make cheap, generic, disposable content easier to produce. That part is real. But artists keep responding like the answer is to keep making generic, disposable posts manually and then demand applause because a human made them.

Human slop is still slop.

A boring TikTok of you lip-syncing in your bedroom is not automatically meaningful because you are a real person. A shaky phone video pointed at a DAW is not raw. Most of the time it just looks like you gave up at the first possible version.

Good lighting matters. Good sound matters. Good framing matters. Editing matters. Cover art matters. A point of view matters.

That does not mean every artist needs a film crew and a $30,000 budget. It means the tools are here. Screen recording exists. Video editing exists. Templates exist. Cheap lights exist. You can make a post feel intentional now without being signed to anybody.

So why are so many people still posting like it is 2011?

The platform is not your career

Because a lot of artists are still treating platforms like they are the industry.

They are not.

TikTok is not your label. Instagram is not your manager. Twitter is not your career. They are rented rooms inside somebody else's building, and the landlord has decided you need to pay to reach the people who already followed you.

So stop building your entire creative life on rented land.

Your social media should lead somewhere. Your Instagram should lead to your music, your videos, your shop, your portfolio, your Patreon, your commission page, your game assets, your whatever. Every platform should connect to the next one. If somebody likes one thing you made, they should be able to find the rest of you immediately.

The work needs a story now

And now, more than ever, people need the story.

AI can make a song-shaped object. It can make an image-shaped object. It can make a video-shaped object.

It cannot tell me why you made yours.

Show the rough version. Show the project file. Show the first bad take. Show the failed attempt. Show what you changed. Show who helped. Show where the idea came from. Show what you were trying to say before you got scared and made it palatable.

The finished piece is not the whole product anymore. You are part of the product. Not in the fake influencer sense. In the human sense.

People want to know there is a person behind the work who actually cares about it.

Stop chasing everybody

And while everybody is fighting for the broadest possible audience, they are missing the easier move: find your people.

You do not need every person on TikTok to love your band. You need the 500 people who are obsessed with exactly what you do. The queer horror-game people. The Black electronic-music people. The retro RPG people. The women who want angry industrial pop. The streamers who need a specific kind of visual package. The indie developers who need usable sprite sheets, UI kits, VFX, rigged models, environments, and assets that actually import into a project.

Fan art is art. Posting drawings is art. But stop confusing “I can make a nice image” with “I have built a product people can use or buy.”

If you want work from game developers, animation studios, video creators, or web teams, make work that survives contact with a real project. Clean files. Clear licenses. Consistent style. Proper exports. Assets that somebody can drag into a game, video, website, or stream package without having to rebuild the entire thing around you.

There is money outside the feed.

Patreon says its discovery systems drive more than $200 million to creators annually. That does not mean everyone needs a Patreon tomorrow. It means direct support is real, and a smaller audience that comes back is worth more than a million random views from people who do not know your name. Patreon: Discovery on Patreon

Get serious

You do not need permission to get serious.

Get your socials in order. Publish your work properly. Know what you own. Copyright begins automatically when original work is fixed, but registration can matter if you need to enforce your rights later. U.S. Copyright Office: What is Copyright?

Make better work. Package it better. Tell people why it exists. Find your niche. Serve it well.

But stop blaming AI for a career you never built.
