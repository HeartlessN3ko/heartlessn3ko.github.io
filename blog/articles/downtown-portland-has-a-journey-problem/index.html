Downtown Portland Doesn’t Have a Destination Problem. It Has a Journey Problem.

Downtown has places worth visiting. The problem is how difficult Portland makes it to arrive, move around, stay, and get home.

By Skye Vernon · Published July 28, 2026

I once took the train to a midnight movie release at the mall. Getting there was easy enough.

Getting home was not.

The movie ended after transit had basically stopped being useful. There were no buses or trains that could get me where I needed to go, and an Uber was surging at around $60. I had made it to the event, but the city had not made it possible to leave it.

That is how downtown Portland feels to a lot of people.

Downtown has restaurants, bars, theaters, hotels, parks, music, food, and events. It is not short on places to go. People do not avoid downtown because there is nothing there.

They avoid it because going there can feel like a hassle from the moment they leave home.

How do I get there? Where do I park? How much is it going to cost? Is there going to be traffic? Can I find the place? Will I be able to get home later without paying a ridiculous Uber price?

If going out feels like a chore, people choose somewhere easier.

It is not one problem

A lot of people blame downtown’s struggles entirely on homelessness, safety, or vacant buildings. Those things are real. Visible homelessness and disorder can make people uncomfortable, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise.

But that is not the whole story.

Downtown’s commercial vacancy rate was 34.7% in late 2024, while Central City foot traffic rose during 2025. People are still coming through downtown. The bigger question is why that activity is not reliably turning into the kind of steady business that keeps restaurants, bars, and shops alive. City of Portland: Central City Vacant Spaces Project

Portland keeps acting like it can promote downtown back to life without making the experience of visiting downtown easier. A good restaurant cannot fix a bad trip there. A great show cannot fix being stranded afterward.

The city shuts down before people are done

Portland has buses, MAX, and Streetcar. That does not mean the system works for a night out.

TriMet has late-night replacement buses on several MAX routes, but they only cover the existing late-night service hours. They do not run all night. TriMet: Late-Night/Early-Morning MAX Service

Portland Streetcar’s published Saturday service ends around 11:30 p.m. Portland Streetcar Development Standards

That is before many concerts, movies, dinner reservations, and nights at a bar are even over.

As an Uber driver, I see the part that comes after that. Once people are out late, their options get expensive fast. They can drive themselves, pay surge pricing, depend on someone else for a ride, or make a bad decision.

The city is not telling anyone to drive drunk. But when public transit is gone and rideshares are expensive, the system is still putting people in a position it should not put them in.

That matters because driving at night, impaired driving, and speeding are serious safety issues in Portland. The City reported that 72% of 2025 traffic deaths happened between dusk and dawn. PBOT and PPB safety release

I am not saying every late-night crash came from somebody leaving downtown. The data does not prove that. I am saying a city that wants people to spend money downtown should give them a safe, realistic way to get home.

Parking and deliveries make everything worse

Parking is part of the problem too.

People going downtown are competing for curb space with residents, buses, rideshares, delivery drivers, bike lanes, loading zones, and event traffic. A person looking for a spot circles the block. A delivery driver cannot find a loading zone and double-parks. Then a rideshare driver has nowhere safe to pull over. Traffic backs up around places people are supposed to be enjoying.

PBOT says double-parked delivery vehicles contribute to congestion and can block both traffic and pedestrian or bike infrastructure. PBOT: Zero-Emission Delivery Zone

This is not about wanting free curb parking everywhere. It is about making downtown easier to use. People should not have to treat finding a legal parking space or loading zone like a scavenger hunt.

Make it easier to have a night out

Portland should try a real late-night plan on Fridays and Saturdays.

Streetcar and the most useful transit connections should run until 3 a.m., with buses timed to take people out of downtown and toward residential areas. SmartPark garages could offer free or validated parking after a certain hour for people visiting downtown restaurants, bars, and events. The city should create clear rideshare pickup zones and make delivery loading zones work instead of letting every block turn into a mess.

Businesses need to meet people halfway too. Too many bars and restaurants charge downtown prices while offering the same generic experience as the place next door. If people are paying to park, dealing with traffic, and figuring out how to get home, there needs to be a reason to stay.

Downtown Portland does not need more people saying, “You should come support us.”

It needs to stop making people work so hard to do it.

Sources: City of Portland: Central City Vacant Spaces Project; TriMet: Late-Night/Early-Morning MAX Service; Portland Streetcar Development Standards; PBOT and PPB safety release; PBOT: Zero-Emission Delivery Zone.
