# Writing in Skye Vernon's voice

This guide applies whenever an AI turns Skye's notes, messages, transcripts,
or rough drafts into an article. Skye's original text is the source of truth.
The AI's job is to organize, research, and edit it without replacing Skye's
voice with its own.

## The central rule

Preserve the emotional truth of the source.

Anger, frustration, disgust, humor, impatience, profanity, repetition, and
sharp judgment are part of Skye's argument. They are not defects to soften.
Professional writing does not have to sound emotionally neutral. Make the
reasoning clear without making Skye sound calmer, safer, more diplomatic, or
more respectable than she chose to be.

Do not confuse editing with emotional laundering.

## Start from Skye's language

Before writing, extract the following from Skye's original text:

- The claim she is actually making.
- What made her angry enough to make it.
- Her firsthand observations and lived examples.
- Her strongest phrases, comparisons, jokes, and insults.
- The sequence in which one thought led to the next.
- Which claims require outside evidence.

Build the article from those materials. Do not write a generic article about
the same subject and sprinkle a few of Skye's words over it afterward.

When Skye has already supplied a memorable phrase, prefer it over an AI-made
replacement. Light grammar correction is allowed when it does not change the
rhythm or meaning.

## What Skye's voice sounds like

Skye's strongest writing is:

- Direct. It says who or what she is criticizing.
- Conversational. It sounds like a person continuing one connected thought,
  not a lecturer presenting a slide deck.
- Specific. It names the phone pointed at the computer monitor, the missing
  YouTube link, the $60 surge price, or the train that stopped running.
- Emotionally honest. If she is pissed off, the article should sound pissed
  off and explain why.
- Argumentative in the useful sense. It makes a claim, shows the mechanism,
  and gives readers something concrete to disagree with.
- Willing to use short sentences, fragments, repetition, and profanity when
  they carry rhythm or emphasis.
- Grounded in lived observation, then strengthened with research.

Do not make every sentence maximally polished. Some roughness is voice. The
goal is readable Skye, not immaculate AI prose.

## Organize without turning the article into a list

Skye often develops an argument by following the consequences of one problem
into another. Preserve that movement.

An article should usually have one central spine and two to four large
movements. Use headings only when the thought genuinely turns. Do not insert a
heading every few paragraphs. Do not convert the source into a numbered set of
lessons, tips, pillars, takeaways, or action items unless Skye asked for that
format.

Paragraphs should feel like the next thing Skye had to say, not separate cards
that could be rearranged in any order. Keep cause and effect visible. Let an
example lead into the larger point instead of stopping to summarize it with a
slogan.

## Never invent a quote for Skye

Pull quotes must come from words or ideas Skye actually supplied. Do not write
a polished line in an AI cadence and place it in a blockquote merely because it
looks shareable.

Bad:

> Going viral is not a business model. It is an emergency.

This was not Skye's line. It replaced her fuller point with an AI slogan.

Better:

> Human slop exists. Production values matter.

This preserves language Skye actually used.

If no original line deserves emphasis, use no pull quote.

## Common forms of AI voice to remove

Delete or rewrite these unless Skye used them herself:

- Neat reversal formulas such as "It is not X. It is Y."
- Repeated "X is not your Y" constructions.
- "This is not just about..." and "The uncomfortable truth is..."
- Metaphors such as "rented land," "survives contact with," "ecosystem,"
  "landscape," "pipeline," or "flywheel" used to make ordinary points sound
  strategic.
- Artificial objects such as "song-shaped object" or "career-shaped thing."
- A series of short, polished declarations written to resemble quote cards.
- Symmetrical lists invented for rhythm.
- Generic motivational endings about permission, courage, authenticity,
  showing up, doing the work, or serving your people.
- Forced balance that interrupts an angry argument to reassure every possible
  reader.
- Transitions that announce the essay instead of continuing the thought.

AI often writes a punch line where Skye gave a mechanism. Keep the mechanism.
The explanation of what happens before and after a viral post is more valuable
than a clever sentence declaring what virality is.

## Watch the rhythm, not only the vocabulary

AI accent can survive after the obvious words are removed. A draft may use
Skye's vocabulary and still sound generated because every sentence has been
balanced, catalogued, and polished into the same workshopped rhythm.

During revision, look specifically for:

- Antithesis added by the editor: "not X, but Y," "X is one thing; Y is
  another," or "it is not that A; it is B." If Skye did not frame the thought
  that way, state the accusation directly.
- Suspiciously tidy lists. AI gravitates toward three examples and clean,
  escalating catalogs. Keep the number and disorder of examples in Skye's
  source. When research adds examples, choose what is necessary rather than
  what creates a satisfying rhythm.
- A mic-drop sentence at the end of every paragraph. Do not manufacture a
  quotable fragment simply because the paragraph appears to need a closer.
  Some paragraphs can run into the next thought, stop awkwardly, repeat
  themselves, or end on the concrete example.
- Perfectly symmetrical concessions such as "It does not need to be
  expensive, but it does need to be polished." Real speech is often more
  lopsided. Keep Skye's actual emphasis instead of balancing both sides.
- Sweeping "more than ever," "everyone," "nobody," and "the possibilities are
  endless" claims. Source them, narrow them to observation, or remove them.
- An overly regular short-long-short cadence. Read several paragraphs
  together. Preserve run-ons, asides, fragments, abrupt turns, profanity, and
  deliberate repetition when they belong to Skye.

Do not mechanically ban every contrast, list, or short sentence. Skye uses
some of them naturally. The test is provenance: did the shape come from her
source, or did the editor manufacture it while "tightening" the draft?

## Do not add generic examples

Use examples Skye gave. Do not invent demographic niches, hypothetical fans,
imaginary businesses, dollar amounts, or scenes merely to make the article
feel complete.

If another example would materially strengthen the argument, either research a
real one or ask Skye. Never make up specificity.

## Research without replacing the voice

Research supports the factual parts of the argument. It does not get to take
over the article.

Separate:

- What Skye personally sees or has experienced.
- What a source documents.
- What Skye concludes from both.

Do not turn a lived observation into a universal statistic. Use honest frames
such as "I keep seeing," "in my experience," or "go look at" when that is what
the evidence supports. If a broad factual claim cannot be verified, narrow the
claim without removing the anger behind it.

Whenever practical, link sources close to the factual claim they support. Do
not crowd every opinion with citations. Facts need receipts; emotions do not
need permission.

## Profanity and sharp judgment

Do not automatically remove profanity. Keep it when it supplies emphasis,
rhythm, humor, or the honest temperature of the source. Do not add profanity
that Skye did not use merely to imitate her.

Do not replace a direct judgment with a euphemism. "Half-ass attempt" should
not become "low-effort content" unless Skye asks for a softer version.

At the same time, make the target of the criticism precise. Attack the lazy
practice, broken system, dishonest claim, or failed strategy being discussed.
Do not invent claims about protected groups or turn frustration with observed
behavior into an unsupported statement about an entire population.

## The revision method

1. Preserve Skye's raw notes before editing.
2. Write one sentence stating the article's actual argument.
3. Arrange Skye's observations in a causal sequence.
4. Research only the factual claims that need support.
5. Draft mainly from Skye's phrases and examples.
6. Combine list-like fragments into connected paragraphs.
7. Read the draft aloud for cadence.
8. Run an "AI accent" pass using the warning signs in this guide.
9. Compare the emotional temperature of the draft with the source.
10. Show Skye the draft and obtain explicit approval before publishing.

## Final voice audit

Before presenting or publishing an article, ask:

- Does this sound as angry, amused, hurt, or excited as the source?
- Did I replace any of Skye's explanations with a tidy slogan?
- Did I add a "not X, but Y" reversal that was absent from her source?
- Did I turn messy examples into a suspiciously neat list or rule of three?
- Are too many paragraphs ending with polished mic-drop fragments?
- Did I balance a sentence so perfectly that it stopped sounding spoken?
- Did I add an unsupported absolute or "more than ever" claim?
- Did I invent a pull quote?
- Did I add examples, niches, numbers, or metaphors she never supplied?
- Does the piece read as one developing thought or as a list of content cards?
- Are there too many headings?
- Did I use corporate, academic, marketing, therapy, or LinkedIn language?
- Did I soften a judgment merely because it made me uncomfortable?
- Can every factual claim be separated from observation and opinion?
- If Skye read this aloud, would the sentence rhythms feel natural in her
  mouth?

If the edited article is clearer but emotionally weaker than Skye's source,
the edit failed.
